If you have not heard of it

What is FIRST Tech Challenge?

A robotics competition for students in grades 7 through 12, run by the nonprofit FIRST. Thousands of teams compete worldwide. Here is the shape of a season.

Every September

A brand new game

FIRST reveals a new challenge with a new field, new scoring, and new rules. Last year’s robot is useless. Everyone starts level.

Then a few months

Design and build a robot

Students do the CAD, machining, wiring, and Java programming. It has to drive itself for the first thirty seconds of every match, then a driver takes over.

Winter into spring

Compete

Qualifiers, league meets, then a regional championship. Teams that go far enough reach the FIRST World Championship. Matches are two and a half minutes, two teams a side.

Judges also award teams on their engineering portfolio, their outreach, and how they mentor others. It is not only about who scores the most points.

August 2026

Our mentorship
program.

We mentor twelve FIRST Tech Challenge teams across North Texas, and we have never charged any of them.

Most teams do not fail because their students are not smart enough. They fail because nobody told them what a portfolio is until the week before judging, or because a motor burned out and there was no spare, or because the one adult who understood the code moved away.

What mentoring actually means

It is rarely a lecture. Most of what we do is small and specific: a spare part driven across town on a Saturday, a second pair of eyes on an autonomous routine that keeps drifting, a portfolio read through before judging with honest notes in the margin. Sometimes it is a call at eleven at night the day before a competition because something snapped and nobody knows what to do next.

We have been the team on the other end of that call. Our first Tech Challenge season was 2023, and we were making the jump from LEGO robots on a four by eight foot board to a metal machine on a twelve by twelve foot field. Java instead of blocks. Machining instead of clicking bricks together. Having made that jump recently is most of why we are useful to teams making it now.

Twelve teams, and counting

Twelve FIRST Tech Challenge teams across North Texas have worked with us so far. Some needed one conversation. Some we have worked with across a whole season. We do not track it as a program with tiers and deliverables, because the moment it becomes that, it stops being the thing teams actually need.

It is free, and it always has been. We are a nonprofit, and mentoring costs us time rather than money.

Starting teams from nothing

The harder and more permanent version of this is helping a family start a team where none exists. We have done it: FLL Techno Raiders exists because we started it.

The bar is much lower than most people think. A team needs four students and two adults, and the adults need no technical background at all. That surprises nearly everyone we tell. We have also presented to more than fifty school administrators on how to stand up a robotics program from scratch, because a school that runs its own team reaches far more students than we ever could visiting once a year.

If your team is stuck

Ask. We would rather answer a question in October than watch a team scratch a season in February. There is no form and no minimum, and you do not have to be near Frisco.

For adults who want to help

You do not need a robotics background for either of these. Teams are short on engineers, and events are short on hands.

Ongoing, a few hours a month

Become a mentor

Mentoring is not a full-time commitment. Most mentors give a couple of hours a month: a design review over a call, a second pair of eyes on some code, or an hour at a build session answering questions.

Machining, CAD, programming, project management, public speaking, and fundraising all matter to a team, and most teams are short on all of them. If you have done any of that professionally, you already know more than enough.

Mentor a team → Mentor roles on firstinspires.org →

One Saturday a season

Volunteer at an event

FIRST events run entirely on volunteers, and they are chronically short. A single qualifier needs judges, referees, field resetters, queuers, scorekeepers, and robot inspectors, often forty or more people for one day.

Most roles need no robotics knowledge and come with training that morning. If you can give one Saturday a season, that is enough to keep an event running.

Volunteer with us → Volunteer roles on firstinspires.org →

And finally

For companies and employers

We are looking for volunteers and for companies willing to help. If your company runs volunteer days, a FIRST event is one of the easiest places to put a group to work.

One qualifier can absorb a large group for a single Saturday. The roles are varied enough to suit anyone in your team, from judging and refereeing to running the field and the scoring table, and none of them need a robotics background.

Your employees spend the day in front of a few hundred students doing real engineering, which is a better story than most volunteer days produce. Tell us roughly how many people and when, and we will tell you which North Texas events need them and put you in touch with the organizers.

If you would rather support a team directly, sponsorship covers parts, travel, and the free workshops we run for schools with no robotics program.

The competition

What FIRST Tech Challenge actually is

FIRST Tech Challenge is a robotics competition for students in grades 7 through 12, run by the nonprofit FIRST. Every September a brand new game is released. Teams have a few months to design, build, and program a robot that competes in qualifiers, league play, a regional championship, and for some teams the World Championship.

A match lasts two and a half minutes, two teams to a side. The robot drives itself for the first thirty seconds, then a student driver takes over. Robots are built from metal, programmed in Java, and play on a twelve by twelve foot field.

Beyond the robot, teams document their engineering process in a portfolio, present to judges, mentor other teams, and run community outreach. Awards are decided as much on that as on match results.
